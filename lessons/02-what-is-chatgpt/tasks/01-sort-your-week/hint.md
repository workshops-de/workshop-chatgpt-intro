<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 1: What counts as "shaping"</summary>

The test: could someone do this task well with only the material in front of them and no knowledge of your company?

- Turning your bullet points into a paragraph → shaping
- Shortening a text you wrote → shaping
- Naming last quarter's revenue → sourcing
- Deciding who gets the budget → judging

</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 2: Finding the seam in a split task</summary>

Take "answer a customer complaint". It breaks into:

1. Understand what the customer wants — shaping, from their message
2. Look up what we promised them — sourcing, needs your systems
3. Decide what we offer — judging, yours
4. Write it politely — shaping

Steps one and four are handed over. Steps two and three are not. Almost every real task looks like this.

</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 3: Describing a good result</summary>

Avoid "a good summary". Try the concrete version:

*Half a page, three bullet points per project, each one naming status, blocker and next step, written so my team lead can read it in two minutes.*

If you can write that sentence, you have already done most of the work of prompting.

</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 4: Naming the concrete failure</summary>

Not "it might be wrong" but the specific damage:

*It would invent a delivery date that sounds plausible, I would paste it into a customer email, and we would miss it.*

That level of specificity is what makes people careful later.

</details>
