<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 1: Picking a task that will succeed</summary>

Good first candidates share a shape: you already have the input, you know what the output should look like, and nobody outside sees the result before you do.

Strong starting points: weekly status reports, meeting notes into decisions, long documents into a briefing, rewriting text for a different audience.

Weak starting points: anything needing today's figures, anything going straight to a customer, anything where you cannot judge the quality.

</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 2: Writing instructions that survive a cold test</summary>

Write them as a briefing for a competent stranger:

*You help me prepare the weekly team update. Input is my rough notes. Output is three sections: done, blocked, next. Maximum 200 words. Neutral tone, no marketing language. Never invent dates, owners or figures — ask me instead.*

The last sentence prevents the failure that would embarrass you.

</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 3: Judging the time comparison fairly</summary>

Count the review as well as the generation. A draft produced in twenty seconds that needs ten minutes of correction has not saved you eight minutes.

Also count the second run, not the first. The first includes the setup you only pay once.

</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 4: Stating the boundary</summary>

A good boundary sentence names both sides:

*I use this for the first draft of the status update and I always verify the dates and the owner names myself. Customer names never go in, because we are on a personal plan.*

This is the sentence to repeat if a colleague asks whether they can do the same.

</details>
