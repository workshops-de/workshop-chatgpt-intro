<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 1: A custom instruction that works</summary>

Something like this, adapted to you:

*I lead a four-person team in technical purchasing at a mid-sized manufacturer. I mostly write for internal colleagues and for suppliers. Answer in German unless I write in English. Keep it short and skip the introduction. If a request is ambiguous, ask before you answer.*

Concrete beats clever. Every detail here is one it no longer has to guess.

</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 2: What not to put in custom instructions</summary>

Not personal data about other people, not customer names, not anything confidential. Custom instructions are attached to every future chat, which multiplies the exposure of anything you put there.

Keep it to how you want to be answered, not to what you are working on.

</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 3: Judging the reasoning comparison</summary>

Do not look for a better-sounding answer. Look for:

- Does it separate the question into steps?
- Does it name assumptions instead of quietly making them?
- Does it raise a risk the fast answer skipped?

If none of that appears, your question was not hard enough to need it. That is a genuinely useful thing to learn about your own work.

</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 4: Good project instructions</summary>

Write the briefing you would give a new colleague who will do this task with you every week:

*This project is for the weekly status report to the management team. Audience is non-technical. Always use the structure status, blocker, next step. Never invent dates or figures, ask me instead.*

That last sentence saves more trouble than any other single line.

</details>
