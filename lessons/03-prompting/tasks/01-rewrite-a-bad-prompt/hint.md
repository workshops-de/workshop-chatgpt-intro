## Nothing of your own in mind?

Pick one of the four materials below and use it for the whole exercise. Each one is deliberately imperfect, which is exactly what you need. English first, German underneath — use whichever your group works in.

<details>
<summary>📄 Material 1: messy meeting notes — good for structuring</summary>

**English**

```text
jour fixe 14 March - present: Anna, Tom, me, Sabine joined late
budget still open, finance said end of month but they said that in February too
Tom: supplier B is cheaper but delivers in 6 weeks, supplier A in 2
we need a decision before the trade fair
Sabine asked about the old contract, nobody knows where it is
someone should check with legal
website texts still not updated, Anna says she has no capacity
Tom mentioned the new tool again, we keep postponing it
next meeting 28 March, maybe move to Thursdays?
also the trade fair booth needs booking, deadline 20 March
```

**Deutsch**

```text
Jour fixe 14. Maerz - anwesend: Anna, Tom, ich, Sabine kam spaeter dazu
Budget immer noch offen, Finanzen sagt Ende des Monats, hiess im Februar auch schon so
Tom: Lieferant B guenstiger, liefert aber in 6 Wochen, Lieferant A in 2
Entscheidung muss vor der Messe fallen
Sabine fragt nach dem alten Vertrag, niemand weiss wo der liegt
jemand muesste die Rechtsabteilung fragen
Website-Texte immer noch nicht aktualisiert, Anna sagt sie hat keine Kapazitaet
Tom hat das neue Tool nochmal erwaehnt, wir schieben das dauernd
naechster Termin 28. Maerz, evtl. auf Donnerstag verlegen?
ausserdem muss der Messestand gebucht werden, Frist 20. Maerz
```

**Suggested task:** turn this into a clean summary with decisions, open questions and who has to do what by when.

</details>

<details>
<summary>🔗 Material 5: a link instead of a pasted text</summary>

You can also point it at a public page you actually need to read — a long article, a press release, a standards document, a competitor's product page.

**Prompt**

```text
Read the page at [URL] and summarise it for [reader], who has to
decide [what]. Before the summary, quote the page heading so I can
see you actually opened it.
```

**Why the quote line matters**

A link is not a guarantee. Depending on the model, the settings and the page, it may never be fetched — and it will produce a confident summary regardless, assembled from the URL and its training data.

Reliably out of reach: anything behind a login or a paywall, your company intranet, and pages whose content only appears after JavaScript runs.

**Use this deliberately as the failure case.** Try a page behind a login as well as a public one, and compare what comes back. It is the fastest demonstration of the whole afternoon.

</details>

## Hints for the exercise itself

<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 1: Naming what is wrong with the first answer</summary>

"Not good" is not actionable. Push to one of these:

- It wrote for a general audience, but mine already knows the product
- It used three paragraphs where I need five bullet points
- It sounds like a brochure and my colleagues do not talk that way
- It named a deadline nobody ever gave it

Each of those maps directly onto one of the five blocks.

</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 2: A rebuilt prompt, as a shape</summary>

*You are writing for [audience], who already know [what].*

*Task: [one verb] the text below.*

*[the actual text, pasted in full]*

*Format: [length], [structure], [language and tone].*

*Do not [the thing that went wrong last time].*

The last line is the one people skip, and it is the one that fixes the recurring annoyance.

</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 3: When to refine and when to start clean</summary>

Refine while the answer is roughly right and needs adjusting. Start a new chat when the conversation has drifted, when you have changed your mind about the goal, or when it keeps dragging in something from ten messages ago.

A long chat carries all its old context. Sometimes that is the problem, not the help.

</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 4: Reading the interview comparison</summary>

Look at which questions it asked. Those are precisely the gaps you would have left in a hand-written prompt.

Most people find the interview version wins on unfamiliar tasks, while a hand-built prompt wins on tasks they repeat, because they already know what matters.

</details>
