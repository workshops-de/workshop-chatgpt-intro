## Overview

Workshops fade fast. This last exercise turns today into four commitments small enough that you will actually keep them, written down while everything is still fresh.

## Prerequisites

- Your notes from the day, especially the use case from Lesson 5

## Steps

### 1. Choose the one task you will actually do

Not a list. One task, the one you built in Lesson 5 or the runner-up from your shortlist.

Write down when in your week it happens, and put the next occurrence in your calendar today.

### 2. Settle one open question with your organisation

Everyone leaves this workshop with at least one: which plan are we on, may I upload customer documents, who decided our data controls, is there a policy at all.

Write down the question and the name of the person you will ask. Send the message before you leave, if you can.

### 3. Pick one thing to retry

Take the attempt you abandoned years ago from the discussion earlier in this lesson, or a task you marked as unsuitable today. Set a reminder for eight weeks out to try it again.

Things move quickly enough that this reminder pays off more often than you would expect.

### 4. Decide how you will keep up

Choose one habit you will genuinely maintain. Thirty minutes a month is plenty:

- Read the release notes of the tool you actually use
- Test one new capability on a task you already know well
- Fifteen minutes with a colleague on what worked, not what was announced

### 5. Write the sentence you will say to colleagues

Someone will ask how the workshop was. Prepare one honest sentence covering both sides: what it genuinely does for you and where you will not let it near your work.

That sentence protects you from being quoted as the person who promised the department a revolution.

## Success Criteria

- [ ] One task chosen, with the next occurrence in the calendar
- [ ] One organisational question written down, with a name attached
- [ ] One retry candidate with a reminder set eight weeks out
- [ ] One keeping-up habit chosen
- [ ] One honest sentence written for colleagues

## Troubleshooting

**"I want to change five things."**
That is exactly why this exercise limits you to one. Note the others somewhere, but commit to a single task. People who start with five typically keep none.

**"My question is for someone who will not answer."**
Ask anyway and write down what happens. An unanswered question about data handling is itself a finding worth reporting.

**"I do not know what to promise my colleagues."**
Then promise nothing and describe instead. "It writes solid first drafts if you give it the material, and it invents sources, so I check everything with a number in it" is more useful than any recommendation.
