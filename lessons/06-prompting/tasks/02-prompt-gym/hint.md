<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 1: Why naming the reader changes everything</summary>

"Summarise this" has no target, so the model aims at the average reader and produces the average summary.

"Summarise this for our finance lead, who needs to decide whether to extend the contract" tells it what to keep and what to drop. Same text, completely different output.

</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 2: Changing one dimension at a time</summary>

A rewrite that asks for shorter, friendlier, simpler and in another language usually returns something that is none of them properly.

Do it in sequence instead. Change the audience, check the result, then shorten. Two messages beat one overloaded one.

</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 3: Getting more out of round 3</summary>

Structuring rewards a second pass more than any other task type:

- "Group these a different way and show me both."
- "Which of these are causes and which are symptoms?"
- "What is obviously missing from this list?"

That last question is the one that most often produces something you had not thought of.

</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 Hint 4: Reading your own comparison</summary>

Most people find that round 1 works best, because the material is complete and the goal is clear. Round 3 usually needs the most follow-ups, because you did not know what you wanted until you saw an attempt.

That is a useful pattern to carry: the vaguer your own goal, the more you should iterate rather than write a longer prompt.

</details>
