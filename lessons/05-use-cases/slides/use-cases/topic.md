# Three questions to pick a use case

1. **Is it mostly text?** Reading, writing, restructuring, translating
2. **Does it come back?** Once a week beats once a year, because the effort amortises
3. **Can I tell good from bad in a minute?** If you cannot judge it, you cannot use it

A task that fails the third question is not a starting point. It is a risk.

# Writing and rewriting

::left::

**Where it saves real time**

- Turning bullet points into a readable draft
- Adjusting tone for a different audience
- Cutting a text to half its length
- Translating and keeping the register

::right::

**How to work**

Give it your rough version, not a blank page.

Editing something mediocre is faster than writing from nothing, for you and for it.

::bottom::
<Callout type="info">
The output is a draft. Treat it the way you would treat a junior colleague's first attempt.
</Callout>

---

# Reading and extracting

- A forty-page report reduced to the five points relevant to your decision
- A meeting transcript turned into decisions, owners and open questions
- A long mail thread summarised before you join it
- A contract scanned for the clauses about notice periods

Ask for the structure you actually need, not for "a summary".

# The underrated one: thinking partner

::left::

**Prompts worth stealing**

- "Poke holes in this plan."
- "What am I not considering?"
- "Argue the case against this."
- "Play the customer who says no."

::right::

**Why it works well here**

There is no fact to get wrong. You are using it for breadth, and you remain the judge.

Rehearsing a difficult conversation with it beats rehearsing alone.

::bottom::
<Callout type="info">
This costs nothing when it is wrong, which makes it the safest place to start.
</Callout>

---

# Learning and explaining

- "Explain this the way you would to someone in my role."
- "Now explain it to a ten-year-old."
- "Quiz me on this until I get five right in a row."

Explaining at the right level is exactly what a language model is built for. Just remember it can be confidently wrong, so learn the shape of a topic here and verify the specifics elsewhere.

---

# Working with your own files

Upload the document, the spreadsheet, the screenshot, and ask about it.

- It reads what you attached instead of guessing from memory
- It can chart, cross-reference and compare
- Numbers still need checking. Ask it to show the calculation and verify the total yourself

<Callout type="warning">
An uploaded file follows the same rule as a pasted one. Check your plan and your policy first.
</Callout>

# When it becomes routine: build a GPT

::left::

**What it is**

A saved assistant: fixed instructions, reference files, one clear job.

Open it and start, instead of re-explaining the context every time.

::right::

**When it pays off**

- The same task at least weekly
- Several colleagues doing it slightly differently
- The context is long and always the same

::bottom::
<Callout type="info">
Build the prompt by hand three times first. Only then do you know what belongs in the instructions.
</Callout>

---

# When the chat window is not enough

At some point someone asks whether this can run automatically, without a person typing.

That is where the API comes in: the same models, addressed by software instead of by a human, so the step disappears into an existing process.

It needs development effort and a different look at data protection, and it is the natural follow-up topic once your manual use cases have proven themselves.

---

# Where it does not belong

- Decisions with legal or personnel consequences
- Anything you would not be able to defend in front of the person affected
- Final figures that nobody recalculates
- Confidential material on a plan that is not cleared for it
- Work where you cannot judge the quality of the result

<Callout type="important">
The tool never carries the responsibility. Your name stays on the output.
</Callout>

# What to take away

- Good candidates are text-heavy, recurring, and easy for you to judge within a minute
- Hand over your rough draft rather than a blank page
- Using it as a thinking partner is the safest place to start, because being wrong costs nothing
- Once a task repeats weekly, move it into a project or a GPT so you stop re-explaining it
- Responsibility does not transfer. Your name stays on whatever goes out
